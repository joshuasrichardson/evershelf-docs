The Prepared Home Field Guide.
Chapter five: Home Preparedness.

A prepared pantry is one part of a prepared home. The full system includes early warning, fire safety, utilities, backup light and power, medication, evacuation supplies, and important documents.

Begin with smoke alarms. The United States Fire Administration recommends alarms inside every bedroom, outside each sleeping area, and on every level of the home, including the basement. Interconnected alarms are valuable because when one sounds, they all sound.

Test smoke alarms monthly. Follow the manufacturer’s battery instructions. Replace the entire alarm according to its instructions and age; the common replacement point is ten years from manufacture. Never disable an alarm because cooking or steam causes nuisance alerts. Use the hush feature, ventilation, or a properly planned location.

Fire extinguishers are tools for small, contained fires when there is a safe escape path. They do not replace evacuation or calling emergency services. Learn the fire classes and choose a listed extinguisher that the user can lift and operate. Place it where it is accessible, not behind the hazard it is meant to address. Seek hands-on training from the local fire department when available.

Know the water shutoff. Identify the valve, make sure the correct tool is accessible if one is needed, and understand when local officials or a qualified professional may direct its use. Label it clearly enough that another household member can find it.

Know the natural-gas shutoff, but be careful with advice. Utah earthquake guidance says to turn off gas only when a leak is suspected, such as a rotten-egg odor, a hissing sound, visible damage, or serious displacement of the building, or when officials direct it. Once shut off, service should be restored by the gas company or another qualified professional. Unnecessary shutoff can create major delays.

Prepare for power outages. Store flashlights and extra batteries. Prefer flashlights to candles. Keep portable power banks charged and rotate them through normal use. Identify medical devices that depend on electricity and create a backup plan with the healthcare or equipment provider.

Generator safety is non-negotiable. Portable generators and other fuel-burning devices must remain outside, far from doors, windows, and vents, according to official instructions. Never use a grill, camp stove, or generator indoors or in a garage. Carbon monoxide cannot be seen or smelled.

Plan for refrigerated medication. Ask the pharmacist or healthcare provider what temperature range is required, how long the medication can remain out of range, and what backup is appropriate. Keep a current medication list, doses, prescribers, pharmacy information, allergies, and essential medical history.

Build a portable seventy-two-hour or evacuation kit for each person. It should be easy enough to carry and specific to the user. Include water, ready-to-eat food, light, radio, batteries, first aid, medication, hygiene, clothing, communication information, and copies of documents. Add infant, disability, sensory, mobility, language, and pet supplies as needed.

Do not let the portable kit become too heavy to move. A home supply and an evacuation supply solve different problems. Large water containers, bulk food, and heavy tools may belong at home, while the go-kit prioritizes mobility.

Protect important documents. FEMA’s Emergency Financial First Aid Kit provides a useful checklist. Include identity, insurance, property, financial, medical, legal, employment, and contact records as appropriate. Store paper copies in a waterproof and fire-resistant location. Keep secure digital backups that can be reached if the home or phone is unavailable. Protect sensitive information with the same care you would use outside an emergency.

Now connect the pieces with a whole-home walk-through.

Start in each sleeping area. Can everyone hear or receive the alarm? Are two exit routes understood? Are heavy objects secured?

Move to the kitchen. Is an appropriate extinguisher accessible? Are flashlights easy to find? Are food and water stored safely?

Locate electrical panels, water shutoff, and gas shutoff. Label them. Keep access clear. Teach the decision criteria, not improvised repair.

Review backup power and carbon-monoxide safety. Review medication and medical-device needs. Pick up every go-kit and confirm that its user can carry it.

Finally, open the document kit and check the update dates.

Every maintained item needs a next review date: smoke alarms, extinguisher inspection, batteries, power banks, medication, water, food, documents, and contact information. A checklist turns good intentions into a dependable routine.
