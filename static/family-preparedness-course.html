1.4USDA FoodKeeperDates · temperatures · cans · refrigeration · freezing+

FoodKeeper is the answer to “Is this still good?” It was developed by USDA’s Food Safety and Inspection Service with Cornell University and the Food Marketing Institute. Look up the specific food—never improvise a safety date.

Never guess. Package dates, storage conditions, food safety, and food quality are different questions. When there is uncertainty, use FoodKeeper or refer the customer to an appropriate authority.

1.5Church food storage resourcesQuantities · rotation · gradual building · avoiding waste+

In Utah, many customers will recognize the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints’ home storage approach. Understand its philosophy without assuming a customer’s membership or beliefs.

The philosophy: begin with foods already in the normal diet, add safe water, continually use and replace a short-term supply, and build longer-term basics gradually as money and space allow. Do not go into debt or buy a system the household will not use.

3.1WaterAmount · duration · rotation · containers+

Start with at least one gallon per person per day for three days; aim for two weeks when feasible. Store more for pregnancy, illness, pets, and hot weather. Commercially bottled water is the most reliable option; user-filled containers should be food-grade, clean, sealed, and replaced every six months.

3.5Oils and fatsOxidation · heat · light · package size · rotation+

Oils are one of the hardest categories because they oxidize and become rancid. Store them cool, dark, and sealed; buy sizes the household will finish after opening; rotate them through normal cooking. “Long-term pantry” does not mean every product is a long-life product.

Common failure: oversized clear plastic bottles stored warm for years. USU notes that plastics allow meaningful oxygen transfer over time, while metal cans resist it better.

Learn to inventory energy and macronutrients, then test whether stored meals work for the household’s life stages, medical needs, allergies, beliefs, and preparation constraints. Needs change for children, pregnancy and breastfeeding, older adults, and many therapeutic diets.

Customer service

Audio chapter 6 · 4:11

Customer Service

Normalize, name the strength, offer a choice, use neutral evidence, and preserve agency.

Download MP3 · 1.9 MBRead transcript
6.1No shame, clear next stepsNormalize · preserve agency · build on strengths+
Avoid
“Wow… You’ve got a lot of expired food.”
Use
“This is actually really common. Most families have supplies that get forgotten over time. The good news is we can build on what you already have.”

A simple response pattern

1 · Normalize

“This happens in a lot of homes.” Lower the social threat without dismissing the issue.

2 · Name the strength

“You already created a starting supply.” Show what can be preserved or reused.

3 · Offer a choice

“Would you rather begin with water, rotation, or the 72-hour kit?” Restore agency.

Use neutral evidence: “The FoodKeeper guidance for this item says…” is better than “I think this is probably bad.” Separate the person from the system: the label, location, container, or reminder failed—not the family.
