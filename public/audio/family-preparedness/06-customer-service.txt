The Prepared Home Field Guide.
Chapter six: Customer Service.

This chapter may matter more than any product recommendation.

Preparedness can carry fear, shame, grief, financial stress, family conflict, and memories of past emergencies. A customer may already feel judged before the first cabinet is opened. Your language can either increase that threat or make useful action possible.

Never make someone feel guilty for the condition of a pantry or kit.

Avoid saying, “Wow. You have a lot of expired food.” That statement centers surprise and judgment. It can make the customer defend past decisions instead of planning the next one.

Try this instead: “This is actually really common. Most families have supplies that get forgotten over time. The good news is that we can build on what you already have.”

That response does three things. It normalizes the situation. It identifies an existing strength. And it opens a path forward.

Use a simple pattern: normalize, name the strength, offer a choice.

Normalize: “This happens in a lot of homes.”

Name the strength: “You already created a starting supply.”

Offer a choice: “Would you rather begin with water, rotation, or the seventy-two-hour kit?”

Choice restores agency. Preparedness advice should not feel like a takeover of the home. Ask permission before opening, discarding, moving, photographing, or labeling. Explain why a change may help. Let the household decide.

Separate the person from the system. If dates are hidden, the visibility system failed. If food is forgotten, the rotation cue failed. If a bin is too heavy, the container choice failed. The customer is not the failure.

Use neutral evidence. Say, “The FoodKeeper guidance for this item says,” rather than, “I think this is probably bad.” Say, “This package requires more preparation water than the current water plan includes,” rather than, “You bought the wrong food.” Evidence reduces personal judgment and improves accuracy.

Do not overwhelm the customer with the ideal version of everything. Identify the smallest meaningful next step. A family may not be able to buy matching containers, two weeks of water, backup power, and a complete food supply at once. Help them prioritize risk, budget, space, and ability.

A low-cost improvement might be moving earliest-use food forward, writing dates with a marker, putting a flashlight by the bed, making a communication card, or adding one gallon of water at a time. Small progress is still preparedness.

Watch for different organizing preferences. One person may need clear containers and visible categories. Another may prefer broad labeled baskets that reduce visual clutter. If the system reflects only the organizer’s preferences, it may disappear as soon as the organizer leaves.

Use curiosity. Ask, “What usually makes this area hard to maintain?” Ask, “Who uses this shelf?” Ask, “What would make replacing this item easier?” Ask, “What foods does your family already cook every week?”

When the answer involves medical, structural, electrical, gas, fire, or nutrition risk outside your scope, say so plainly and respectfully. Referring to a qualified professional is good customer service.

Practice difficult moments in advance.

If a customer is embarrassed, slow down and normalize.

If a customer is afraid, acknowledge the concern and return to one concrete action.

If family members disagree, clarify the shared goal and offer options instead of choosing sides.

If money is tight, remove urgency from purchases and focus on low-cost actions, rotation, and gradual building.

If a customer wants certainty you cannot provide, use the source and state the boundary.

Here is the exercise. Take five judgmental statements and rewrite each one using normalize, strength, and choice. Then say the new version out loud. Written empathy can still sound stiff when spoken. Practice until the language feels natural.

The customer should finish a visit feeling more capable than when it began. Dignity is not an extra feature of the service. It is part of preparedness, because people maintain systems they understand, choose, and believe they can manage.

End of chapter six.
