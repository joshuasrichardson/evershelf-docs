The Prepared Home Field Guide.
Chapter one: Master These First.

This is the foundation of the course. Before learning products, storage systems, or long-term food, learn the public guidance that every recommendation should rest on.

Preparedness is not about predicting every disaster. It is about making ordinary decisions before stress makes them harder. Who will contact whom? Where will the family meet? What will each person need? How will everyone leave, shelter, or reconnect? What supplies will make the first hours and days safer?

Begin with FEMA Ready, at Ready dot gov. Use it as your baseline for the question, “What should every family have?” The answer begins with a household emergency plan, a family communication plan, an evacuation strategy, and a maintained supply kit tailored to the people and animals in the home.

A useful communication plan has at least two meeting places. One is immediately outside the home, for something like a house fire. The second is outside the neighborhood, in case the area is closed or evacuated. It also names an out-of-area contact. During a regional emergency, a distant contact may be easier to reach than someone nearby. Every household member should have the contact information both on a phone and on paper.

The plan must include real household needs. Think about children, older adults, disabilities, language needs, medication, medical equipment, transportation, work and school locations, and pets. A generic checklist is only a starting point. The final plan has to fit the actual family.

Next, study the American Red Cross. The Red Cross turns preparedness into clear household actions. Its survival kit guidance covers water, nonperishable food, flashlights, a battery or hand-crank radio, extra batteries, first aid, medication, hygiene, tools, and copies of important documents.

For water and food, the Red Cross describes a three-day evacuation supply and a two-week home supply. For water, the common planning amount is one gallon per person per day. That amount may need to increase for hot weather, illness, pregnancy, pets, or other needs.

The Red Cross is also a strong practical source for first aid kits, power outages, winter storms, and wildfires. Notice how its hazard pages are structured: what to do before, during, and after. Use that pattern when explaining risk. Give people the next action, not a wall of frightening possibilities.

For Utah households, study Be Ready Utah, the public preparedness program of the Utah Division of Emergency Management. Local knowledge makes recommendations more credible and more useful.

Utah preparedness should include earthquakes. Practice Drop, Cover, and Hold On. Secure tall furniture and heavy objects. Keep sturdy shoes and a flashlight near the bed. Know where utilities are located and when they should, and should not, be turned off.

Also plan for winter storms, power outages, flooding from rain or runoff, wildfire evacuation, and wildfire smoke. Smoke is not only a visibility problem. Fine particles can be especially harmful for children, older adults, pregnant people, and people with heart or lung conditions. Utah’s air-quality resources explain how to reduce exposure and how to monitor conditions.

Now learn the USDA FoodKeeper. You will often hear, “Is this still good?” Do not guess. FoodKeeper provides storage guidance for hundreds of foods and beverages. It helps separate four questions that people often mix together: What does the package date mean? How was the food stored? Is this a food-safety issue? Or is it mainly a quality issue?

A best-by date is not automatically the same thing as a safety deadline. But that does not mean every old product is safe. Condition, temperature, package integrity, contamination, and the specific food matter. Look up the item. During a power outage, use official temperature and time guidance. Never recommend tasting food to decide whether it is safe.

Finally, understand the food-storage philosophy taught by the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints. This is culturally relevant in Utah, but never assume a customer’s faith or membership.

The practical philosophy is broadly useful: start with food the household already eats. Add safe drinking water. Build gradually as money and space allow. Continually use and replace the short-term supply. Add longer-term staples carefully. Avoid waste, extremes, and debt.

The phrase “store what you eat and eat what you store” captures the rotation principle. A supply that never enters ordinary meals is more likely to be forgotten. A plan that depends on unfamiliar ingredients or cooking skills is more likely to fail under stress.

Here is the chapter exercise.

First, draft a family communication plan with two meeting places and one out-of-area contact.

Second, compare your household kit with the Red Cross checklist.

Third, identify two earthquake hazards in one room.

Fourth, look up five foods in FoodKeeper.

And fifth, write a gradual three-month plan using foods the household already eats.

The goal is not fear. The goal is a calm, repeatable baseline. Master these sources first, and let them anchor everything that follows.

End of chapter one.
