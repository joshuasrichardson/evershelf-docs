The Prepared Home Field Guide.
Chapter three: Food Storage.

Learn food storage by category. For every category, ask the same questions. What does it contribute? What shortens its life? How should it be packaged? What does it require to prepare? How will the family rotate it? And will they actually eat it?

Begin with water. Store at least one gallon per person per day for three days. Aim for two weeks when feasible. Utah’s Be Ready program recommends a two-week minimum. Store more for pregnancy, illness, pets, physical exertion, high altitude, and dry or hot conditions.

Unopened commercially bottled water is a reliable emergency source. For user-filled water, use clean, food-grade containers that close tightly. Do not reuse containers that held toxic chemicals. The CDC recommends replacing user-filled water every six months. Store water cool, dark, and away from chemicals. Use a mix of sizes, because a fifty-five-gallon barrel is efficient but extremely heavy and not portable.

Think through access before filling a large container. How will it be drained? Is there a siphon? Can smaller containers be refilled from it? Could an earthquake damage the location? Water planning is not complete until the family can actually dispense the water.

Next are grains: rice, wheat, oats, and flour.

These foods do not all behave the same. White rice generally stores longer than brown rice because brown rice retains oils that can become rancid. Whole wheat berries usually store longer than milled whole-wheat flour. Flour exposes more surface area and has a shorter practical storage life. Heat and moisture speed deterioration. Insects and rodents are physical threats. Packaging and the condition of the food at the start matter.

Utah State University Extension is a strong source for grain and packaging guidance. Food-grade buckets add physical protection, but a plain plastic bucket is not automatically a long-term oxygen barrier. Mylar-style foil laminate bags can improve protection from moisture and oxygen when properly sealed. Oxygen absorbers can be appropriate for sufficiently dry foods in suitable barrier packaging. Never add them casually to moist foods. Follow a tested food-specific source.

Keep portions manageable. Several smaller sealed bags inside a bucket can be easier to rotate and can limit the damage if one package fails. Label the food, package date, and any treatment used.

Now consider protein. Dry beans and lentils can store well, but they require water, time, and cooking fuel. Older beans may take longer to soften. Ready-to-eat canned meat, fish, or beans require less fuel but add weight and have different storage limits. Peanut or seed butter is energy-dense and familiar but contains fats that limit storage life. Dried eggs and freeze-dried meat can add variety, but opened-package instructions and cost matter.

Build more than one protein path. If the power is out and fuel is limited, a plan based entirely on dry beans may be impractical. If a family has allergies, dental limitations, or texture needs, some products may not work. Test meals before depending on them.

For dairy, powdered milk is common. Protect it from moisture, light, oxygen, and heat. Package size matters. A large can may appear economical, but after opening it may be difficult for a small household to finish within the manufacturer’s recommended time. Choose sizes that match realistic use.

Practice using powdered milk in ordinary recipes: baking, sauces, soups, oatmeal, or drinks. If no one likes it or knows how to use it, it is not yet part of a dependable plan.

Oils and fats are one of the hardest categories. They are nutritionally important and energy-dense, but they oxidize and become rancid. Heat, light, air, and time are the enemies. Store oils cool, dark, and sealed. Buy package sizes that the household can finish. Rotate them through normal cooking.

Do not assume that putting oil in a long-term pantry makes it a long-life product. Utah State University notes that plastic packaging permits oxygen transfer over time. Some barrier materials resist oxygen better, but no package removes the need for rotation. The best oil plan is often a smaller working supply with reliable replenishment.

Now consider freeze-dried foods. Customers may already own Mountain House, Augason Farms, ReadyWise, or products from the former Thrive Life brand. Learn these products for identification and label literacy, not endorsement.

Read the actual package. Record the product, lot information, best-by date, package condition, allergens, calories, protein, fat, sodium, preparation water, cooking method, and instructions after opening. Do not confuse servings with meals or with adequate calories. A bucket can advertise many servings while still providing fewer daily calories than a person expects.

Calculate the water required to prepare freeze-dried food. That water is in addition to drinking and sanitation needs. Consider whether hot water is required and what fuel will heat it. Taste-test a few products before buying a large amount. Familiarity and morale matter in stressful conditions.

Across every food category, use three layers.

Layer one is everyday rotating food: familiar products the family already uses.

Layer two is emergency-ready food: items that need little water, fuel, or preparation.

Layer three is longer-term storage: carefully selected dry or freeze-dried products in appropriate packaging.

Avoid building one layer while ignoring the others.

Here is the chapter exercise.

Calculate three-day and two-week water needs for the household.

Compare white rice, brown rice, wheat berries, oats, and flour by storage characteristics and normal use.

Build one no-grid day of protein-containing meals.

Make one recipe using powdered milk.

Audit oil package size, light, heat, and rotation.

Finally, compare four freeze-dried labels by total calories, protein, sodium, allergens, preparation water, and after-opening life.

Food storage is not a contest to own the oldest food. It is a living household system. Safety comes from reliable sources. Value comes from rotation. Resilience comes from variety, preparation practice, and a plan the family can actually use.
