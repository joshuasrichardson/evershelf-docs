The Prepared Home Field Guide.
Chapter four: Nutrition.

The central question is simple: Can this family actually live on what is stored?

You do not need a nutrition degree to notice obvious gaps. You do need to understand the limits of your role and know when to refer a customer to a registered dietitian, physician, pharmacist, or other qualified healthcare professional.

Begin with usable calories. Emergency food marketing often emphasizes servings. A serving is a label unit, not proof of a complete meal and not proof of enough daily energy. Add the calories in the amount the person will actually eat. Then calculate the total supply for the household.

Calorie needs vary with age, body size, sex, activity, pregnancy, breastfeeding, health, and environment. The USDA Dietary Reference Intake calculator can provide estimates. Use estimates as planning tools, not as medical prescriptions.

Next, look at protein. Does the plan contain practical protein sources across the day? Are they compatible with allergies, religious practices, vegetarian or vegan diets, chewing ability, and available cooking fuel? A pantry may contain many grain calories but very little protein variety.

Then look at fat. Fat provides concentrated energy and helps with essential functions and the absorption of certain vitamins. Yet fats and oils can be difficult to store for long periods. A sound plan includes rotating sources the household normally uses instead of depending on a forgotten oversized bottle.

Carbohydrates are often the easiest category to accumulate because rice, wheat, oats, pasta, flour, and sugar are familiar and affordable. They are valuable. But a room full of grain is not automatically a balanced diet. Pair staples with protein, fat, fruits, vegetables, fiber, and appropriate micronutrient sources.

Now consider life stages.

Children are not small adults. Their energy, nutrient, texture, choking-safety, and emotional needs differ by age. Include familiar foods and ways to prepare them safely. Infants may require formula, feeding supplies, safe water, and very specific handling. Never improvise formula dilution.

Pregnancy and breastfeeding can change energy, nutrient, hydration, and food-safety needs. A pregnant customer with questions about supplements, medical risk, or a restricted diet should review the plan with a clinician.

Older adults may need attention to protein, hydration, medication interactions, chewing or swallowing, mobility, and easy-open packaging. A food is not useful if the person cannot safely open, prepare, chew, or digest it.

Special diets need more than a separate labeled bin. Ask about food allergies, intolerances, celiac disease, diabetes, kidney disease, heart conditions, texture-modified diets, and religious or ethical food choices. Do not diagnose or prescribe. Document the requirement, prevent cross-contact where relevant, and ask the household’s healthcare team to review the emergency menu when clinical needs are involved.

Water and cooking fuel belong in the nutrition audit. A dry menu may look adequate on paper but require more water and energy than the family has stored. Sodium also matters, especially when many meals are canned or packaged. Again, do not prescribe a sodium target for a medical condition, but do recognize when a menu is heavily dependent on high-sodium products and needs professional review.

Use the current Dietary Guidelines for Americans and USDA life-stage materials as broad references. Use the Nutrition Facts label for the actual product. Use a healthcare professional for individualized clinical advice.

Here is a simple one-day audit.

Write down every meal, snack, and drink.

Record the amount each household member would eat.

Add total calories and protein.

Identify sources of fat, carbohydrate, fiber, fruits, and vegetables.

Calculate all preparation water and fuel.

Check allergens, sodium, texture, package opening, and refrigeration after opening.

Then ask whether the menu fits every person in the household.

If the answer is uncertain, do not hide the uncertainty. Say, “Because this involves a medical or life-stage need, I recommend reviewing this emergency menu with your healthcare team or a registered dietitian.”

Preparedness advice becomes safer when it has clear boundaries. Your job is to help the household see the inventory, identify practical gaps, use reliable public guidance, and bring the right professional into the conversation when needed.
