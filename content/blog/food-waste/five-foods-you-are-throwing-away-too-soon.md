**The average American family throws away over $1,500 worth of food every year, and a significant portion of that is food that was still perfectly safe to eat.** Here are five foods that almost everyone discards too early — and how to know when they're actually bad.

## Key Takeaways

- **Eggs** last 3-5 weeks past the sell by date (use the water float test)
- **Yogurt** is good 1-2 weeks past the date when sealed and refrigerated
- **Canned goods** last 2-5 years past the printed date
- **Bread** that's stale isn't spoiled — freeze it or repurpose it
- **Condiments** last months to years longer than most people assume

## The 5 Foods You're Wasting

### 1. Eggs

**How long they actually last:** 3-5 weeks past the sell by date

Eggs are one of the most frequently wasted foods because people treat the sell by date as a safety deadline. It's not — it tells the store when to rotate stock.

**The water test:** Fill a bowl with cold water and gently place the egg in it.

| Result | What It Means | Action |
|--------|---------------|--------|
| Sinks, lies flat on side | Very fresh | Use anytime |
| Sinks, stands upright | Older but safe | Use soon — great for hard boiling |
| Floats | Gas buildup, likely spoiled | Discard |

Why this works: as eggs age, moisture escapes through the shell and is replaced by air. More air = more buoyancy. A floating egg has enough gas buildup that it's not worth the risk.

### 2. Yogurt

**How long it actually lasts:** 1-2 weeks past the date (unopened, refrigerated)

That thin layer of liquid on top? That's whey — it's supposed to be there. Stir it back in. Yogurt's live cultures actually help preserve it by keeping harmful bacteria in check.

**Signs yogurt has actually gone bad:**
- Unusual sour smell (beyond its normal tanginess)
- Pink, green, or dark spots (mold)
- Excessive liquid that's cloudy or off-colored

### 3. Canned Goods

**How long they actually last:** 2-5 years past the date (for most items)

Canned food is one of the safest, longest-lasting foods in your pantry. The date printed on the can is a quality guideline from the manufacturer. As long as the can is in good condition, the food inside is safe.

**When to actually discard canned food:**
- The can is dented along the seam (side seam or where the lid meets the body)
- The can is bulging or swollen
- The can is rusted through
- Food spurts or foams when opened
- Contents smell off when opened

A dent on the body of the can (not the seam) is cosmetic — the food is fine.

### 4. Bread

**How long it actually lasts:** 5-7 days at room temperature, 3-6 months frozen

Americans throw away roughly 40% of the bread they buy. Most of it goes stale, not bad. Stale bread is safe — it's just lost moisture.

**Before you throw bread away, consider:**
- **Toast it** — heat restores texture
- **Make breadcrumbs** — pulse stale bread in a food processor, store in the freezer
- **French toast or bread pudding** — stale bread actually works *better* because it absorbs more liquid
- **Croutons** — cube, toss with olive oil and salt, bake at 375°F for 10 minutes
- **Freeze it fresh** — if you won't finish a loaf in time, freeze half immediately. Frozen bread toasts perfectly straight from the freezer

**When bread is actually bad:** Visible mold — any color. Don't just cut the mold off; bread is porous and mold threads spread throughout.

### 5. Condiments

**How long they actually last:**

| Condiment | Opened, Refrigerated |
|-----------|---------------------|
| Ketchup | 6-12 months |
| Mustard | 12 months |
| Soy sauce | 2-3 years |
| Hot sauce | 6 months - 2 years |
| Mayonnaise | 2-3 months |
| Relish | 9-12 months |
| Worcestershire sauce | 2-3 years |
| Vinegar | Essentially indefinite |

The high acidity, salt content, and/or sugar in most condiments makes them inhospitable to bacterial growth. Ketchup that's been open for 8 months is fine. That bottle of soy sauce from two years ago? Still good.

**The only condiment to be careful with:** Mayonnaise, because it contains eggs and oil. Even so, commercial mayo (which contains vinegar and preservatives) lasts much longer than homemade.

## How Much Money Could You Save?

If the average family wastes $1,500 per year on food, and even 20-30% of that waste comes from prematurely discarded items like these, that's **$300-$450 per year** that stays in your pocket by simply learning what "bad" actually looks like.

The common thread: trust your senses more than the date stamp. Look, smell, and (if it passes those tests) taste. Dates are guidelines, not deadlines.
