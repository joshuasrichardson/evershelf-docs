**Having food in storage and being prepared are not the same thing.** A closet full of rice means nothing if your family develops scurvy in week three because nobody thought about vitamin C. Real preparedness means covering every nutritional base, knowing exactly how many days your supply will last, and rotating stock so nothing expires unused on a shelf. This guide gives you the framework to do it right.

## Key Takeaways

- **Minimum targets:** 3 days (FEMA baseline), 14 days (covers most regional emergencies), 30-90 days (serious preparedness)
- **Measure readiness directly:** Track water, calories, protein, essential fats, micronutrients, and no-cook food instead of forcing every item into one nutritional category
- **Rotation is non-negotiable** — a food supply you don't rotate is a food supply that will fail you when it matters
- **Calculate by calories:** 2,000 cal/day per adult, multiplied by people, multiplied by days
- **Storage conditions matter more than the food itself** — every 10 degrees F above 70 degrees F roughly halves shelf life

## How Much Food Do You Need?

Forget vague advice about "having some extra food around." You need a number. Here is how to calculate it.

**The formula:** 2,000 calories per adult per day x number of people x number of days = total calories needed.

A family of four building a 30-day supply needs roughly 240,000 calories of stored food. That sounds like a lot, but a single 50-pound bag of white rice contains about 82,000 calories. Three bags of rice and you are halfway there — for under $60.

### Storage Tiers

| Tier | Duration | What It Covers | Estimated Cost (Family of 4) |
|------|----------|---------------|------------------------------|
| **FEMA Minimum** | 3 days | Power outages, short-term disruptions | $50-$75 |
| **Regional** | 14 days | Hurricanes, winter storms, supply chain interruptions | $200-$400 |
| **Standard** | 30 days | Extended grid-down, prolonged regional events | $400-$800 |
| **Extended** | 90 days | Severe economic disruption, long-duration events | $1,000-$2,000 |

These costs assume you are buying bulk staples at grocery store prices, not premium freeze-dried kits from preparedness retailers. You can cut those numbers further by buying on sale and building gradually.

**Adjust for your household.** Children under 12 need roughly 1,200-1,800 calories per day. Elderly or sedentary adults can estimate 1,600-1,800. If you are doing physical work during an emergency — clearing debris, hauling water, chopping wood — bump the estimate to 2,500-3,000 calories per day. Underestimating caloric needs during high-stress, high-labor situations is a serious planning error.

## Building Balanced Food Readiness

Most people who start building a food supply make the same mistake: they buy whatever is cheap and shelf-stable without thinking about nutritional balance. You end up with 200 pounds of rice, no protein, no fat, and nothing with vitamin C. That is not a food supply — it is a recipe for malnutrition.

Organize stored food with familiar pantry categories, then measure what the inventory actually provides. A jar of peanut butter belongs with proteins for browsing, while simultaneously contributing calories, protein, fat, and no-cook readiness to the preparedness audit. The sections below cover the major kinds of food worth stocking.

### 1. Grains and Starches

**Purpose:** Your primary calorie source — energy to keep moving, thinking, and functioning.

**What to store:**

| Item | Shelf Life (Sealed, Room Temp) | Calories per Pound | Notes |
|------|-------------------------------|-------------------|-------|
| White rice | 25-30 years | 1,650 | The backbone of any long-term supply |
| Hard winter wheat berries | 30+ years | 1,500 | Requires a grain mill to use |
| Rolled oats | 25-30 years (in Mylar) | 1,750 | Versatile — oatmeal, baking, thickener |
| Dried pasta | 8-10 years | 1,680 | Easy to cook, minimal water needed |
| White flour | 5-8 years (sealed) | 1,650 | Shorter life than whole grains |
| Cornmeal | 5-10 years (sealed) | 1,640 | Cornbread, polenta, tortillas |

White rice is the gold standard for emergency grain storage. It is cheap, calorie-dense, has an extraordinary shelf life, requires only water and heat to prepare, and nearly everyone will eat it. Start here.

Wheat berries are the next step up for serious preparedness. They store even longer than rice and give you the ability to mill fresh flour for bread — but only if you also store a hand-crank grain mill. Without the mill, wheat berries are useless.

### 2. Proteins

**Purpose:** Muscle maintenance, immune function, wound healing. Without adequate protein, your body starts breaking down its own muscle tissue within days.

**What to store:**

| Item | Shelf Life | Calories per Pound | Notes |
|------|-----------|-------------------|-------|
| Dried beans (pinto, black, navy) | 25-30 years | 1,550 | Must be cooked — requires fuel and water |
| Dried lentils | 25-30 years | 1,500 | Cook faster than beans, no soaking needed |
| Canned chicken/tuna/salmon | 3-5 years | 500-700 | Ready to eat, no cooking required |
| Peanut butter | 2-5 years (unopened) | 2,660 | High calorie, shelf-stable, no prep needed |
| Freeze-dried meat | 25-30 years | 800-1,200 | Expensive but extremely long-lasting |
| Protein powder (whey/plant) | 1-2 years | 1,600-1,800 | Supplement, not a primary source |

Dried beans and lentils are the most cost-effective protein for long-term storage — pennies per serving with decades of shelf life. The catch is they require water and cooking fuel, so factor that into your plan.

Canned meat is the protein you will reach for first in any emergency because it is ready to eat straight from the can. Rotate it into your regular cooking (canned chicken works in casseroles, tuna in sandwiches) and replace what you use.

Peanut butter deserves special mention. At roughly 2,660 calories per pound with significant protein and fat, it is one of the most calorie-efficient foods you can store. It also requires no cooking, so one jar improves several readiness measures at once.

### 3. Fats and Oils

**Purpose:** Calorie density and essential fatty acids. Fat contains 9 calories per gram — more than double the 4 calories per gram in carbohydrates or protein. In a calorie-scarce situation, fat is survival.

**What to store:**

| Item | Shelf Life (Unopened) | Notes |
|------|----------------------|-------|
| Vegetable oil | 1-2 years | Best all-purpose cooking oil for storage |
| Coconut oil (refined) | 2-3 years | Solid at room temperature, long life |
| Shortening (Crisco) | 2-3 years | Excellent for baking, high calorie |
| Olive oil | 1-2 years | Shorter life, but versatile |
| Ghee (clarified butter) | 1-2 years (shelf-stable) | Butter flavor without the dairy spoilage |

Fat is the most commonly underestimated category in emergency food storage. A diet of rice and beans without added fat will leave you constantly hungry and chronically low on calories. You need cooking oil to make meals satisfying and calorie-sufficient.

Store at least 1-2 gallons of vegetable oil per person for a 90-day supply. Keep it sealed, in a dark location, and rotate it on a 12-18 month cycle — oil goes rancid faster than most dry goods.

### 4. Baking, Cooking, and Flavorings

**Purpose:** Preservation, cooking, calorie supplementation, and morale. Never underestimate morale.

**What to store:**

| Item | Shelf Life | Notes |
|------|-----------|-------|
| Granulated sugar | Indefinite | Store dry, sealed, away from moisture |
| Honey | Indefinite | May crystallize — still safe, warm to reliquefy |
| Maple syrup (real) | Indefinite (unopened) | Refrigerate after opening |
| Salt (iodized or canning) | Indefinite | Essential for cooking, preservation, electrolytes |
| Brown sugar | 2-4 years | Hardens but is still usable |
| Molasses | 2-5 years | Adds iron and flavor |

Salt is arguably the single most critical non-caloric item in your supply. Beyond flavoring, salt is essential for preserving meat, fermenting vegetables, maintaining electrolyte balance, and oral rehydration during illness. Store at least 5 pounds per person for a 90-day supply, more if you plan to preserve food.

Honey is the perfect emergency sweetener. It is calorie-dense (1,382 calories per pound), antibacterial, and literally never expires. Archaeologists have found edible honey in Egyptian tombs. Crystallized honey is not spoiled — set the jar in warm water and it will return to liquid form.

Sugar and sweeteners also serve a psychological function that is easy to dismiss until you need it. During extended emergencies, the ability to make something that tastes good — a cup of sweetened tea, oatmeal with honey, a simple syrup on pancakes — has a measurable effect on morale and mental resilience.

### 5. Fruits and Vegetables

**Purpose:** Vitamins, minerals, fiber, variety, and morale. Vitamin C is one useful readiness measure, but a resilient pantry includes a broad mix of fruit and vegetable sources.

Scurvy symptoms begin in as little as 4-6 weeks without vitamin C: fatigue, swollen gums, joint pain, slow wound healing, and eventually tooth loss and internal bleeding. It was the leading cause of death on long sea voyages for centuries. The fix is simple — you just have to plan for it.

**What to store:**

| Item | Shelf Life | Vitamin C per Serving | Notes |
|------|-----------|----------------------|-------|
| Vitamin C tablets (500mg) | 3-4 years | 500mg | Cheapest, longest-lasting option |
| Canned tomatoes | 2-5 years | 20-30mg per cup | Cooking staple with decent vitamin C |
| Citric acid powder | Indefinite | Varies by use | Adds tartness, some vitamin C benefit |
| Freeze-dried fruit | 25-30 years | Varies | Retains most nutrients, lightweight |
| Canned fruit (in juice) | 2-5 years | 15-40mg per serving | Rotate regularly |
| Tang or drink mix (vitamin-fortified) | 2-3 years | 100% DV per serving | Cheap, easy, palatable |
| Rose hip powder | 1-2 years | Very high | Niche but extremely potent |

Vitamin C tablets can provide inexpensive backup coverage, but they do not replace the fiber, variety, and broader micronutrients supplied by fruits and vegetables.

Beyond supplements, canned tomatoes pull double duty as a cooking ingredient and vitamin C source. A cup of canned tomatoes provides roughly 20-30mg of vitamin C — not a full daily requirement (65-90mg for adults) but a meaningful contribution when combined with other sources.

Freeze-dried fruit is the premium option. It retains the majority of its original vitamin content, lasts 25-30 years in sealed containers, and provides variety that boosts morale. Freeze-dried strawberries, mangoes, and pineapple are excellent sources.

### 6. Dairy and Alternatives

**Purpose:** Calcium, protein, familiar cooking ingredients, and meal variety.

Powdered milk, shelf-stable milk, cheese powder, and suitable plant-based alternatives can make stored grains and prepared foods much more useful. Choose products your household already tolerates and rotate them according to package guidance.

### 7. Prepared Meals, Snacks, and No-Cook Foods

**Purpose:** Food that remains usable when fuel, power, time, or cooking water is limited.

Include canned meals, nut or seed butter, crackers, shelf-stable bars, canned fish, and other foods that can be eaten immediately. These are pantry categories for finding food; EverShelf also counts their calories toward the separate No-Cook Readiness measure.

### 8. Water

**Purpose:** Hydration, cooking, sanitation. You will die from dehydration in 3 days. You will not die from hunger in 3 days. Water is the most important item in your emergency supply.

**How much:** 1 gallon per person per day, minimum. That covers drinking and basic cooking. If you need water for sanitation, first aid, or pets, increase to 1.5-2 gallons per person per day.

| Duration | 1 Person | Family of 4 |
|----------|----------|------------|
| 3 days | 3 gallons | 12 gallons |
| 14 days | 14 gallons | 56 gallons |
| 30 days | 30 gallons | 120 gallons |

**Storage options:**

- **Store-bought bottled water** — rotate every 1-2 years, store in a dark location away from chemicals
- **Food-grade water containers (5-7 gallon)** — stackable, portable, easy to rotate
- **55-gallon water barrels** — the most space-efficient bulk option, treat with water preserver concentrate (good for 5 years)
- **WaterBOB** — a 100-gallon bladder that fits in your bathtub, meant to be filled when you know a storm is coming

**Purification methods to store alongside water:**

- Unscented liquid bleach (8 drops per gallon, let stand 30 minutes)
- Water purification tablets (follow package instructions)
- Portable water filter (Sawyer Squeeze, LifeStraw, or similar)
- Rolling boil for 1 minute (3 minutes above 6,500 ft elevation)

Having stored water is ideal, but having the ability to *make* water safe is the real long-term solution. A quality gravity water filter or portable filter handles situations where your stored water runs out but you have access to a natural source.

## Where to Store It

The three enemies of stored food are heat, moisture, and light. Everything else is secondary.

**Temperature is the single biggest factor.** The general rule is that for every 10 degrees F above 70 degrees F, you cut the shelf life of stored food roughly in half. Rice stored in a climate-controlled closet at 70 degrees F lasts 25-30 years. That same rice in a garage that hits 100 degrees F in summer may degrade in 5-8 years.

**Best storage locations:**

- Interior closets (consistent temperature, no exterior wall exposure)
- Basement or cellar (naturally cool, but watch for moisture)
- Under beds (uses dead space, stays relatively cool)
- Dedicated pantry or shelving in a temperature-controlled room

**Worst storage locations:**

- Garage (temperature swings, pests, moisture)
- Attic (extreme heat in summer)
- Outdoor sheds (no climate control, pest access)
- Near water heaters, furnaces, or south-facing walls

**Spread your supply across multiple locations.** If a pipe bursts and floods your basement, you do not want 100% of your food supply in that basement. Keep your primary supply in one location and a smaller backup somewhere else — a bedroom closet, a storage unit, or a relative's house.

## Rotation Strategies

The best emergency food supply in the world is worthless if everything in it expired two years ago. Rotation is what separates a real preparedness plan from an expensive pile of garbage.

### FIFO: First In, First Out

When you add new items, put them behind the older items on the shelf. Always pull from the front. This is the same system every grocery store uses, and it works.

### Integrate Into Regular Cooking

Your emergency food should not sit untouched in a closet. The canned chicken in your supply is the same canned chicken you can use in a casserole on Tuesday night. Use it, then replace it on your next grocery run. This way your supply is constantly cycling through fresh stock.

Items to integrate into weekly cooking:
- Canned meats and fish (in sandwiches, casseroles, pasta dishes)
- Canned tomatoes and vegetables (in soups, sauces, chili)
- Rice and pasta (regular side dishes)
- Cooking oil (daily use)
- Peanut butter (snacks, sandwiches, baking)

### Quarterly Checks

Set a recurring reminder — every January, April, July, and October — to physically inspect your supply. Check for:

- Items within 3 months of expiration (move to the kitchen for immediate use)
- Damaged cans (dented seams, rust, bulging)
- Signs of pests (droppings, chewed packaging, webs)
- Moisture or condensation on containers
- Temperature at the storage location

## Common Mistakes

### 1. Nutritional Imbalance

The number one mistake is buying 300 pounds of rice and calling it done. Rice provides calories, but very little protein, fat, or vitamin C. A true emergency food supply needs measurable coverage across energy, macronutrients, micronutrients, water, and foods that remain usable when cooking is impossible.

### 2. Buying and Forgetting

People spend $500 on emergency food, stack it in the garage, and never look at it again. Five years later, half of it is expired, the cans are rusted, and mice have chewed into the pasta boxes. A food supply requires active management — rotation, inspection, and replacement.

### 3. Not Tracking Dates

If you do not know when something expires, you cannot rotate it. Every item in your supply needs a visible date — either the manufacturer's date or a date you wrote on it with a marker when you stored it. EverShelf handles this digitally, giving you a single dashboard view of everything in your supply sorted by expiration date, but even a marker and a notebook is better than nothing.

### 4. Ignoring Water

People obsess over food and completely neglect water. You can survive weeks without food. You cannot survive 72 hours without water in most conditions. Water should be the first thing you store, not an afterthought.

### 5. Storing in Hot Locations

The garage is the default dumping ground for emergency supplies, and it is one of the worst places to store food. Summer garage temperatures can exceed 120 degrees F in many climates. At those temperatures, canned goods degrade rapidly, oils go rancid, and shelf life predictions become meaningless. Store food where you would be comfortable sitting — if it is too hot for you, it is too hot for your food.

### 6. No Cooking Plan

You stored 50 pounds of dried beans but have no way to cook them without electricity. Dried beans require 1-4 hours of simmering. Do you have a camp stove, fuel, and a pot? A supply of food you cannot prepare is not a supply — it is inventory. For every food item you store, ask yourself: "How do I cook this if the power is out?"

## Getting Started: A Simple First Purchase

Here is a concrete 72-hour emergency kit for a family of four. Everything on this list is available at a regular grocery store, and the total cost is approximately $50-$60.

### The $50 Starter Kit

**Grains and Starches**
- 10 lb bag of white rice — $8
- 2 lb box of pasta — $2
- 1 canister of rolled oats (42 oz) — $5

**Proteins**
- 6 cans of chicken or tuna (5 oz each) — $8
- 2 cans of black beans (15 oz each) — $2
- 1 jar of peanut butter (16 oz) — $4

**Fats and Oils**
- 1 bottle of vegetable oil (48 oz) — $4

**Baking, Cooking, and Flavorings**
- 1 bag of sugar (4 lb) — $3
- 1 container of salt (26 oz) — $1
- 1 bottle of honey (12 oz) — $5

**Fruits and Vegetables**
- 1 bottle of vitamin C tablets (100 count) — $5
- 2 cans of diced tomatoes (14.5 oz each) — $2

**Water**
- 4 one-gallon jugs of water (3-day supply at minimum allocation) — $5

**Total: approximately $54**

This kit provides roughly 2,000 calories per person per day for 72 hours, along with water and a mix of protein, fat, micronutrients, and ready-to-eat options. It is not gourmet. It is not a 90-day supply. But it gives you a practical foundation to build on.

### What to Do Next

1. Buy the starter kit above on your next grocery trip
2. Store it in a cool, dark location inside your home
3. Write today's date on every item with a permanent marker
4. Next month, buy a second round and extend to a 7-day supply
5. Continue adding each month until you reach your target duration

Building a food supply is not a one-time purchase. It is a habit. Add a few items every grocery trip, rotate what you have, and track it so nothing slips through the cracks.
